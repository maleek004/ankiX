I almost gave up on building my first real software product before I even typed `git init`.

Coming from a background of self-directed data engineering projects, I transitioned to full-stack software engineering with intense curiosity. I had ideas. I had problems I wanted to solve for people. But like any beginner, I didn't have all 10,000 details of building a production full-stack platform memorized.

Then modern AI coding assistants arrived. To me, they looked like an incredible force multiplier—a patient, tireless pair programmer that could help me bridge the knowledge gap and translate my ideas into running software.

Right as I was about to unleash my tinkering mind, I slammed into the online discourse:

> *"AI is for senior engineers only."*  
> *"If you use AI as a beginner, you’ll never learn how to code."*  
> *"You're just an AI copy-paster producing mediocre slop."*

The fear was real. The emotions online were raging. For a few days, imposter syndrome froze me in my tracks.

Then I realized something crucial: **The problem isn’t using AI as a beginner. The problem is using AI lazily.**

If you prompt an LLM, blindly paste its output, and refuse to read the diffs or understand the architecture, you will build fragile toys and suffer from catastrophic syntax amnesia. You won't learn.

So I chose to reject the dogma. I decided that beginners shouldn't avoid AI—they should embrace it immediately, but **do it right**.

I call my approach **Religiously Vibe Coding**.

I let AI provide the 10x scaffolding velocity, but I hold myself to hardcore architectural discipline. I audit every database index, trace every async state machine, and battle-test every failure mode. And when the AI introduces a concept or syntax I don't know, I don't just skim it—I commit it to long-term memory.

In fact, to hold myself accountable, I'm using AI to build **AnkiX** ([ankix.tech](https://ankix.tech))—a platform designed to help engineers defeat syntax amnesia through active recall and in-browser runnable code micro-exercises.

Yes, it’s meta: using AI to build the very platform that ensures you never become dependent on AI.

This publication is my public lab notebook. I'm going to document the entire journey—the architectural wins, the nasty AI hallucinations, the security measures, and the exact mechanics of building a production system in the age of generative models.

No guru filters. No "10 prompts to make you rich." Just raw, honest building in public.

## 💼 3. LinkedIn Post

The most pervasive advice given to junior and transitioning engineers right now is:  
*"Don't touch AI until you already know how to code."*

Coming from self-directed data engineering into full-stack software engineering, this advice almost stopped me in my tracks. The fear of becoming a "mediocre AI copy-paster" induced immediate imposter syndrome.

Then I took a hard look at how software is actually evolving.

AI isn't going away. Telling an aspiring builder to ignore LLMs today is like telling an accountant in the 1980s to ignore spreadsheets. 

The issue isn't using AI—it's **using AI lazily**.

If you use AI as an excuse not to understand the code, you'll build fragile toys and suffer from severe syntax amnesia. But if you pair AI velocity with first-principles discipline, it becomes the greatest interactive engineering tutor on Earth.

I call this **Religiously Vibe Coding**:
- Move fast with AI-assisted scaffolding.
- Ruthlessly audit every PostgreSQL connection, EF Core query, and security boundary.
- Deeply learn and retain the syntax you just shipped.

To put my philosophy to the test, I'm building **AnkiX** ([ankix.tech](https://ankix.tech))—a platform dedicated to helping developers retain code syntax and concepts through active recall and browser-runnable micro-exercises.

I’m kicking off a short, serial publication documenting the raw reality of building a production system in public with AI—the architecture, the hallucinations, and the security hurdles.
